What I found

The current app already has a distinctive world: paper, ink, a geometric mark, recurring characters, illustrated Paris, and a notebook of personal discoveries. The recent La Une pass has simplified the home screen. The remaining problem is consistency across the experience: editorial labels, multiple type voices, heavy rules, decorative counters, and different controls still compete with the learner’s task.

01 · TOO MUCH TO DECODE

The metaphor does too much work.

La Une, L’Épreuve, Le Lexique, Le Relevé, and Le Courrier are evocative, but a newcomer has to decode what each one does. Keep French names as chapter or activity titles. Give navigation plain labels in the learner’s language.

02 · TOO MANY VISUAL VOICES

Everything wants to be a headline.

The current source mixes serif, sans-serif, and monospace, with italics, tracked uppercase, varying weights, and several size scales. Use one UI family. Let hierarchy come from size, space, and contrast.

03 · TOO MUCH FURNITURE

Let the activity breathe.

The lesson composition can show round, mode, count, concept, motif, rule, prompt, choices, confidence, and correction together. Reveal those at the moment they help: one question, one answer area, one next action.

04 · PLAY WITHOUT PRESSURE

Reward a real little moment.

Use the existing story characters and places. A warm acknowledgement, a flower, and a small postcard earned from the story feel at home here. Keep corrections kind and progress truthful.

The visual system

Manrope throughout, with regular and bold weights. Soft ink on clean, warm paper. Vermilion has one job: the next meaningful action. Sage means progress and reassurance. Cobalt stays secondary. The original four-shape mark survives with softer corners.

What to take from Duolingo

Take the clarity of the next step, the stable control language, and the relationship between characters and learning. Duolingo describes its guided home experience as a way to make the next step clear, and its illustration system is deliberately coherent. Atelier can apply those principles while keeping its own quieter, literary world. Home design rationale · Typography guidelines · Illustration principles.

How this carries into the real app

  1. Agree on the direction using this preview. Validate with a few new learners: can they find the next activity, finish a question, and revisit a saved word without explanation?
  2. Introduce shared tokens and controls. Consolidate the existing La Une, Épreuve, Cahiers, and Courrier component treatments into those primitives.
  3. Roll out the shell and home, then practice and feedback, then reader, conversation, and notebook. Preserve the existing learning APIs, SRS, corrections, and session state.
  4. Localise controls to the learner’s language. Keep French in the learning content. Carry the current Missions and Studio entry points into Speak with a simple activity selector; keep written missions available.
  5. Validate real new-user, returning-user, loading, empty, retry, offline, long-text, large-font, and dark-mode states before release. Use real duration, due counts, and progress instead of the authored sample data.

Preview scope

This is an isolated, light-theme design prototype. The current app screens and authentication are unchanged. Lesson feedback, six-word review, notebook search and filters, reading, translations, keepsakes, and a two-turn conversation are interactive locally. Conversation replies are scripted; voice recording and AI services are not connected. The eight-minute estimate, learner profile, dates, and initial streak are sample content. Progress lasts only until reload. Native device QA, dark mode, full adaptive curricula, and backend integration belong to implementation.

Inspection used the current frontend source, the running mobile QA page’s current home/lesson/notebook compositions, the existing artwork, and the recent overhaul notes. Older captured screens were used only as historical context, not as evidence of the current authenticated account.
